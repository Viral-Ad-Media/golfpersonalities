Golf has been played for centuries, but the game we know today is the product of many inventions. Some changed the sport for good. Others are just fun gifts. Here's a look at the most important.

The ball

  • The feathery: early golf balls were leather pouches stuffed with boiled goose feathers. They were expensive and slow to make, and they fell apart when wet.
  • The gutta-percha ball (around 1848): the "guttie" was made from the dried sap of a Malaysian tree. It was cheap and durable, and it made golf affordable for far more people. Players noticed that scuffed gutties flew better, which led to deliberately textured surfaces.
  • The rubber-core ball (1898): Coburn Haskell and Bertram Work, of the B.F. Goodrich company, created a ball with rubber thread wound around a solid core. It flew much further than the guttie and quickly took over.
  • Dimples: early in the 20th century, dimpled covers became standard because they help the ball stay in the air longer.
  • Solid multi-layer balls (around 2000): modern solid-core balls largely replaced wound balls. They combine distance with spin control.

The clubs

  • Steel shafts: they replaced hickory in the 1920s and 30s. The USGA approved them in 1924 and the R&A in 1929.
  • The sand wedge: Gene Sarazen is credited with developing the modern sand wedge, with a flange that glides through sand. He used it on his way to winning the 1932 Open Championship.
  • Metal woods: in the late 1970s and 1980s, metal-headed "woods" began replacing persimmon. Today's drivers are made of titanium and carbon composite.
  • Graphite shafts: lighter than steel. They became popular from the 1970s onwards, especially in drivers.
  • Cavity-back irons: perimeter weighting made irons far more forgiving for everyday golfers.

Around the course

  • The wooden tee: before tees, golfers built small mounds of wet sand. In 1899, Dr. George Franklin Grant, an African-American dentist in Boston, patented a wooden tee.
  • The golf cart: electric carts appeared in the mid-20th century and changed how many people play, especially in the United States.
  • GPS and laser rangefinders: instant, accurate distances to the flag have sped up play and improved club selection.
  • Launch monitors and simulators: radar and camera systems measure ball speed, launch and spin. They've transformed coaching and club fitting.

The fun side: novelty golf gadgets

Not every invention is meant to change the game. Golf gift shops are full of playful gadgets: putting sets you can use in the bathroom, ball retrievers that telescope across ponds, putters with built-in levels and floppy "trick" clubs for laughs on the range.

From feathers stuffed in leather to radar tracking every shot, golf's inventions show how much the game has changed, while its basic challenge stays the same.
